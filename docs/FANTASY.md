# Fantasy basics

What a reasonably intelligent fantasy player knows, written down so RosterBots' AI, the
bench that grades it, and Claude in this repo are all held to it. Started Nov 6 2026. The
owner's word is the source: a section is **settled** once the owner has said it, and
**draft** until then.

This is judgment, not rules. Hard limits stay code: the No-Cray Rules for the game-day add
(`AI-NATIVE.md`, `packages/core/src/outside.ts`), and Roster Advice's own rules once they are
written (owner, Nov 7: it "need not act on the same rules as the lead-up to the game";
`DECISIONS.md`). This page decides what is a *good* suggestion inside them.

## The test

> "We're not trying to avoid getting it wrong, we will get it wrong at times. But we don't
> want to do anything the reasonably intelligent human would think is insane." (owner, Nov 6)

It cuts both ways. Too reckless (drop the wrong player, wreck a user's season) is the worst
failure; too careful (so safe it almost never does anything) is a close second. Every change
to the AI is judged on both: fewer insane moves, without more insane misses.

## Roster construction basics (settled, owner, Nov 7)

Written by the owner after the waiver bench, where eight of ten models suggested a backup
tight end (owner: "asinine"). Kept word for word.

### Fantasy Football Roster Construction Basics: Universal Principles for Fantasy Football Leagues

#### 1. Understand the League Before Evaluating the Roster

Roster construction must begin with the league's actual settings.

Determine:

- Scoring format: standard, half-PPR, full-PPR, tight-end premium, points per first down, or custom.
- Starting lineup requirements and number of FLEX positions.
- Whether the league uses one quarterback, Superflex, or two quarterbacks.
- Number of bench and injured reserve (IR) slots.
- Roster limits and positional restrictions.
- League size and available replacement talent.
- Waiver rules, transaction limits, and acquisition costs.
- Redraft, keeper, dynasty, or best-ball format.

Never assume conventional roster settings apply to every league.

#### 2. Prioritize Starting-Lineup Strength

The primary purpose of roster construction is to maximize expected production from the starting lineup over the relevant competitive period.

A player who meaningfully improves a starting position is generally more valuable than a backup who is unlikely to play.

Evaluate roster depth in relation to actual starting requirements, not simply the number of players at each position.

A team starting three wide receivers and two FLEX players may need substantially more receiver depth than a team starting two receivers and one FLEX.

#### 3. Understand Positional Scarcity

Player value depends partly on the quality of available alternatives.

In traditional one-QB leagues, replacement quarterbacks are often relatively accessible, making excessive quarterback depth inefficient.

In Superflex and two-QB leagues, starting quarterbacks are much scarcer and often deserve substantial roster investment.

In tight-end premium leagues, tight-end depth and high-volume tight ends may become more valuable.

Positional scarcity must be evaluated within the actual league, not according to universal rankings.

#### 4. Do Not Automatically Roster Backups at Every Position

A fantasy roster does not need a backup at every starting position.

In typical redraft leagues:

- Quarterback: A second quarterback is optional in one-QB formats, depending on starter reliability and replacement availability.
- Running back: Bench depth is generally valuable because injuries and changes in workload can create major opportunities.
- Wide receiver: Depth is valuable, particularly in PPR and multi-FLEX leagues.
- Tight end: A backup is not automatically necessary when the starter is healthy and productive.
- Defense: Carrying one defense is usually sufficient in leagues where defenses can be streamed.
- Kicker: Carrying one kicker is usually sufficient.

A missing positional backup is not inherently a roster deficiency.

Recommend adding one only when its expected value exceeds the opportunity cost of the roster spot.

#### 5. Treat Bench Spots as Valuable Assets

Every bench position has an opportunity cost.

Bench players should ideally provide at least one of the following:

- Credible starting value.
- Injury protection at an important position.
- Significant upside if circumstances change.
- Coverage for a foreseeable lineup problem.
- Trade value.
- Strategic value during an upcoming stretch of the season.

Avoid filling bench spots with low-upside players who are unlikely to enter the starting lineup.

An available bench spot also has value because it allows the manager to respond quickly to new opportunities.

#### 6. Favor Upside When the Starting Lineup Is Strong

When a roster already has reliable starters, bench construction should generally emphasize players capable of becoming meaningful contributors.

Examples include:

- Running backs one injury away from a major workload.
- Young receivers earning increasing playing time.
- Players returning from injury with substantial potential roles.
- Talented players whose opportunities may expand.

A low-ceiling player with a slightly higher weekly projection is not automatically a better bench asset than a player with substantial breakout potential.

Balance upside against the team's actual need for immediate production.

#### 7. Evaluate Running Back Depth by Opportunity

Running back value is strongly influenced by workload, team situation, and injury exposure.

Consider:

- Expected carries and targets.
- Goal-line opportunities.
- Passing-down involvement.
- Backfield competition.
- Injury history and current health.
- Offensive environment.
- Whether a backup would inherit meaningful work if the starter became unavailable.

Do not assume every backup running back is a valuable handcuff.

A backup with a clear path to a substantial workload can be more valuable than a nominal starter in a limited committee role.

#### 8. Evaluate Wide Receiver Depth by Role and Opportunity

Receiver depth should reflect starting requirements and expected usage.

Consider:

- Target volume and target share.
- Routes run and playing time.
- Offensive role.
- Quarterback situation.
- Red-zone involvement.
- Recent changes in opportunity.
- Scoring format.

In PPR leagues, consistent target volume may be especially valuable.

In standard scoring, touchdown opportunities and yardage upside may carry relatively more importance.

Do not rely solely on projected fantasy points or roster percentage.

#### 9. Use Injured Reserve Slots Efficiently

IR eligibility and capacity must be evaluated according to league rules.

When a player is IR-eligible and an eligible IR slot is empty, moving that player into IR may create a usable bench position without dropping anyone.

Before recommending a drop or addition, determine:

- Total IR capacity.
- Number of occupied IR slots.
- Which rostered players are eligible.
- Whether the platform permits the proposed move.
- Whether the roster is subject to restrictions involving ineligible players in IR.

An IR move may be more valuable than an unnecessary player swap.

Do not recommend dropping a valuable injured player solely because the player is unavailable this week.

#### 10. Plan for Bye Weeks Without Overreacting

Bye weeks are predictable and should be considered in roster planning.

However, a future bye does not automatically justify acquiring another player immediately.

First determine whether the roster already contains suitable replacements.

Consider:

- How many starters share a bye week.
- Whether existing bench players can cover those positions.
- How far away the bye occurs.
- Whether replacements are likely to remain available.
- The opportunity cost of using a roster spot early.

Avoid sacrificing valuable long-term depth to solve a distant, temporary problem.

#### 11. Treat Kickers and Defenses Differently From Skill Positions

In most conventional leagues, kickers and defenses are replaceable positions.

For defenses, prioritize:

- Upcoming opponents.
- Opposing offensive efficiency.
- Sack and turnover opportunities.
- Injuries affecting the opposing offense.
- League-specific defensive scoring.

For kickers, consider:

- Offensive scoring opportunities.
- Field-goal volume.
- Weather and playing conditions.
- Scoring rules.

Do not recommend carrying multiple kickers or defenses simply to provide backup coverage.

A second defense can be justified by an unusually favorable future schedule or limited waiver availability, but it must offer enough value to justify the roster spot.

#### 12. Account for League Size and Waiver-Wire Quality

Depth requirements change with league size.

In smaller leagues, replacement players are generally more accessible, making bench flexibility especially valuable.

In deeper leagues, viable replacements may be scarce, increasing the importance of retaining usable depth.

A player who is easily replaceable in an eight-team league may be a valuable asset in a sixteen-team league.

Evaluate every acquisition relative to the actual available free-agent pool.

#### 13. Adapt to Redraft, Keeper, Dynasty, and Best Ball

**Redraft:** Prioritize current-season production, playoff usefulness, and short-term opportunities.

**Keeper:** Consider current production alongside future keeper eligibility, cost, and potential value.

**Dynasty:** Account for player age, long-term opportunity, future seasons, rookie picks, and competitive window.

**Best ball:** Prioritize total roster production, weekly upside, positional depth, and lineup optimization through the league's automatic scoring rules. In many best-ball formats, in-season transactions are unavailable.

Do not apply redraft roster-building assumptions blindly to dynasty or best-ball leagues.

#### 14. Account for Team Competitive Position

Roster construction should reflect whether the team is:

- Contending for a championship.
- Fighting to reach the playoffs.
- Comfortably positioned for the postseason.
- Rebuilding in a dynasty or keeper format.

A contender may reasonably favor immediate reliability over speculative long-term upside.

A rebuilding dynasty team may prioritize younger assets and future value.

A team with a strong playoff position may accept short-term uncertainty to improve its postseason ceiling.

#### 15. Evaluate Trades and Waiver Moves by Net Roster Value

A transaction should improve the roster's overall competitive position, not merely one individual position.

Consider:

- Starting-lineup improvement.
- Depth gained or lost.
- Positional scarcity.
- Replacement options.
- Injury and bye-week implications.
- Future upside.
- Opportunity cost.
- League-specific scoring.

Do not recommend a transaction simply because the incoming player has a higher projected point total.

A marginal upgrade may not justify sacrificing a valuable bench asset.

#### 16. Distinguish Necessary Moves From Optional Improvements

Not all roster issues deserve immediate action.

Classify potential moves as:

- **Urgent:** An empty starting position, an unavailable starter without coverage, or a significant opportunity likely to disappear.
- **Beneficial:** A meaningful improvement in starting strength, depth, or upside.
- **Optional:** A modest improvement with limited immediate impact.
- **Unnecessary:** A transaction that adds little value, duplicates existing coverage, or wastes roster flexibility.

Do not manufacture transactions simply to provide recommendations.

Sometimes the optimal roster-construction decision is to make no move.

#### 17. Preserve Manager Intent and Transaction Safety

Roster advice must respect the manager's choices and the application's permitted actions.

- Do not automatically replace a healthy starter solely because another player has a slightly higher projection.
- Do not recommend dropping a valuable player without considering their role, health, and future value.
- Do not treat a player as expendable solely because they are currently injured.
- Do not assume a transaction is legal without checking roster and league rules.
- Do not invent roster openings, IR eligibility, or available free agents.
- Verify that player identities and transaction IDs match the intended recommendation.

Roster advice should assist the manager rather than override their strategy without justification.

#### 18. Use Current Information and Recognize Uncertainty

Roster construction depends on information that changes throughout the season.

Relevant information includes:

- Injuries and practice participation.
- Starting-role changes.
- Trades and signings.
- Suspensions and availability.
- Coaching decisions.
- Depth-chart changes.
- Recent player usage.

Distinguish confirmed facts from projections, speculation, and uncertain reports.

When current information is essential to a recommendation, verify it through an appropriate source rather than relying solely on historical knowledge.

#### 19. Optimize for the Relevant Time Horizon

A player's value changes depending on the period being evaluated.

Consider immediate weekly production, upcoming bye weeks, remaining regular-season games, fantasy playoff schedules, and future seasons when applicable.

A player with strong rest-of-season projections may still be a poor immediate acquisition if the team needs a starter this week.

Conversely, a player with limited immediate value may be an excellent stash for the fantasy playoffs.

#### 20. The Fundamental Principle

Every roster spot should serve a purpose, and every transaction should improve the team's expected competitive position.

Good roster construction is not about achieving equal positional depth, filling every backup position, or maximizing projected points in isolation.

It is about allocating limited roster spots toward the most valuable combination of starting production, useful depth, upside, flexibility, and future opportunity under the league's actual rules.

When no available move provides a meaningful improvement, retaining the current roster is a valid and often optimal decision.

## Defenses (settled, owner, Nov 6)

The owner's specifics for basics §11.

- **Defenses stream.** You pick one week to week; you don't hold one for the season.
- **Rest of season barely counts.** When suggesting a new defense, its rest-of-season ranking
  and projection sit very low on the list. In the owner's words: "I don't want to say at
  all, but not much."
- **The one exception is the No. 1 ranked defense being available.** It usually isn't, and
  even the No. 1 defense isn't worth that much.

## Injury news (owner, Nov 6 and 7: the owner's judgment, not official NFL reporting)

- **Acting on news takes a confirmed out**, "or a wild confidence score that a player will in
  fact be out" (owner, Nov 6).
- **Read what the team does, not only what it says, and weigh every reason it might do it.**
  After Lamar Jackson's thumb surgery the Ravens gave a high 5th-round pick, a 7th two years out
  and $2.5M for one year of Jacoby Brissett. The owner's first read: "It's a keep hope alive
  move"; a rebuilding team, or one that has given up on the season, wouldn't pay that. Then the
  other readings (owner, Nov 7): they could believe Brissett can win it all, they could be
  posturing for fans, and the GM and coaches could be worried about their jobs. A cheap move
  fits several stories, so it shifts the odds a little and settles nothing.
- **When holding is free, don't bet on the reading.** A player in an IR spot costs nothing
  unless another injured player needs it; keep the upside whichever story is true.
- **A "season likely over" headline is not a confirmed out.** On Nov 7 GLM suggested dropping
  Lamar on a betting site's "season likely over," built on the coach's "potentially." The
  owner: not a terrible decision, he very well could be done, but in the owner's read the trade
  says the Ravens believe he may be back ("that's my take, not an official NFL story"). Brissett
  sat in both leagues' free-agent lists as a Raven, and GLM's own source mentioned the trade; no
  model connected the two.

## Where the app disagrees today

Not changed yet; each is the owner's call. Section numbers are the basics above.

- **The AI isn't told all of the league's settings (§1, §12, §14).** Since Nov 7 (not
  deployed) it gets the room: bench and IR spots, who qualifies for IR, whether an add needs
  a drop, so it can see an empty IR spot like the one beside Mike Evans on Nov 7. Still
  missing: the scoring format, league size, waiver rules and the team's record.
- **The rules count projected points, which §15 says not to do alone.** No-Cray rule 7 lets an
  add through only if ESPN projects him higher for the rest of the season than the player
  dropped. That blocks stashing an injured star (§6, §19): ESPN projects Davante Adams at 0,
  so on Nov 7 it refused the stash the owner then made. For defenses it is the number the
  owner says barely counts.
- **The Advisor sees each defense's rest-of-season projection with no caveat.** On Nov 6, Opus
  suggested the Rams D/ST on rest of season alone (63 against the Saints' 34).
- **"Never drop a playing starter" blocks the most normal defense move**, a straight swap of
  your starting defense for a better matchup. For Roster Advice this rule goes (Nov 7).
- **Fixed Nov 7, not deployed: the instructions invited filler (§4, §16).** One kind of
  suggestion was defined as "a position with no backup at all", which is how backup tight
  ends, kickers and defenses got suggested. It now reads as a real construction problem, a
  missing backup not one by itself, and the Advisor never invents a move: when none is worth
  making it says so, with what it checked and what to watch.
- **Fixed Nov 7, not deployed: nothing checked that the words match the move (§17).** A Kimi
  suggestion said "add Dalton Schultz" while its move added Tyreek Hill, and the app would
  have offered the tap. The app now throws out any suggestion whose words name other players
  than its move, and works out the roster's room itself before the AI sees it (§1, §9 in
  part: bench and IR spots, who qualifies for IR, whether an add needs a drop).
- **"Out for the season" takes any cited source (§18).** In Roster Advice the AI's own reading
  of a web report can mark a player out for the season, which lifts the rules that protect him.
  On Nov 7 that was a betting site's headline.
- **The bench doesn't grade against these basics yet.** It checks the app's rules only.
